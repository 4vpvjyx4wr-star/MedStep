1. Which of the following best describes the palpatory quality of a
Chapman point?
A. Large, ropy band of muscle
B. Small, firm, smooth, bead-like nodule
C. Diffuse boggy edema
D. Bony exostosis
Answer. B. Small, firm, smooth, bead-like nodule
Explanation. Chapman points are classically 2 to 3 mm, firm, smooth,
and round, with sharp pinpoint tenderness. Larger ropy structures
suggest muscle hypertonicity; boggy edema suggests acute tissue
change; bony exostoses are osseous rather than fascial.
2. Which feature best distinguishes a Chapman point from a trigger

B. Chapman points are non-radiating; trigger points refer pain along
characteristic patterns
C. Chapman points lie in skeletal muscle; trigger points lie in fascia
D. Chapman points are treated with thrust; trigger points are not
Answer. B. Chapman points are non-radiating; trigger points refer pain
along characteristic patterns
Explanation. Trigger points typically refer pain along myofascial
patterns; Chapman points are sharply pinpoint and non-radiating, and

3. What is the dominant proposed mechanism of Chapman points?
A. Direct lymphatic blockage
B. Central sensitization with facilitated sympathetic outflow
C. Local trauma with scar tissue formation
D. Autoimmune fascial inflammation
Answer. B. Central sensitization with facilitated sympathetic outflow
Explanation. The modern model proposes that persistent visceral
afferent input facilitates dorsal-horn interneurons, amplifying
sympathetic efferent activity and altering local vasomotor tone and
lymphatic drainage. The original lymphatic blockage hypothesis has
been largely superseded.
4. In what sequence are Chapman points generally treated when both
anterior and posterior points are present?
A. Posterior before anterior
B. Anterior before posterior
C. Whichever is more tender first
D. Simultaneously, never one before the other
Answer. B. Anterior before posterior
Explanation. Anterior points are usually easier to palpate and are
treated first by convention. Posterior points are reassessed after
anterior treatment.

Write a concise paragraph or list. Model answers follow each item.
1. Define a Chapman reflex point. Include the typical palpatory
characteristics, anatomical layer, and the diagnostic requirement that
distinguishes a true reflex from a single tender finding.
Model answer. A Chapman reflex point is a small, firm, smooth, bead-
like nodule of tissue-texture abnormality found in the deep fascia or
periosteum, classically 2 to 3 mm in diameter and described as a
ganglioform contraction. It elicits sharp, pinpoint, non-radiating
tenderness and is reproducible across visits. A true Chapman reflex
requires the presence of both an anterior and a corresponding posterior
point at the established anatomical location for a given organ system.
2. Explain the dominant neurophysiologic model of Chapman points,
including the role of spinal facilitation, the autonomic system, and
lymphatic drainage. Note the limits of the evidence.
Model answer. Persistent visceral afferent input from a diseased or
irritated organ produces subthreshold excitation of dorsal-horn
interneurons at the segmentally corresponding spinal level. Over time,
these interneurons become facilitated and respond to progressively
smaller inputs. Facilitation amplifies sympathetic efferent outflow,
thereby altering vasomotor tone and lymphatic drainage in the
associated fascial tissues. The palpable point is the somatic expression
of this circuit. Treatment is proposed to feed back through the same
pathway, modulating autonomic tone and improving regional lymphatic
clearance. The model is mechanistically coherent and pedagogically
useful, but direct experimental confirmation in humans remains limited.

3. Describe the six steps of the Chapman point treatment procedure,
including position, identification, engagement, dose, sequence, and
reassessment.
Model answer. Position the patient comfortably (supine for most
anterior points; prone or seated for posterior points). Identify the point
by gentle palpation at the expected anatomical location, looking for a
firm, well-circumscribed, bead-like nodule with sharp localized
tenderness. Engage the tissue with the pad of one or two fingers,
applying gentle, focused circular rotary pressure. Does the treatment
last 15 to 60 seconds, or until the nodule softens and tenderness
diminishes? Sequence the treatment by addressing anterior points
before posterior points when both are present. Reassess the point after
treatment: reduced tenderness and softened texture indicate response;
persistence may signal ongoing visceral dysfunction or a need to
reconsider the working diagnosis.
4. List five common clinical indications for Chapman point assessment
and treatment, and explain how the technique fits within a broader
osteopathic plan.
Model answer. Indications include functional gastrointestinal disorders
(reflux, IBS; genitourinary complaints UTI, dysmenorrhea, ovarian
dysfunction); cardiopulmonary symptoms (arrhythmia, bronchitis,
asthma exacerbation); endocrine concerns such as thyroid dysfunction;
and supportive care in systemic conditions with fatigue, autonomic
imbalance, or chronic inflammation. Chapman’s work serves as a
diagnostic adjunct corroborating visceral hypotheses generated from
history and review of systems, as a low-force entry point to modulate
autonomic tone and support lymphatic flow, and as a monitoring tool
across visits. It is best integrated with broader techniques such as
lymphatic pumps, myofascial release, soft tissue work, or visceral
approaches.

5. List the absolute and relative contraindications to Chapman point
treatment.
Model answer. Absolute contraindications: lack of informed consent;
absence of a palpable Chapman point on examination; localized
infection, open wound, malignancy, or recent surgical incision at the
treatment site. Relative contraindications: severe acute illness where
palpation may exacerbate symptoms; fragile skin or connective tissue
disorders; significant patient anxiety or poor tolerance of palpation;
anticoagulation with bruising risk over the treatment site.

Appendix: tip of the twelfth rib. Kidneys: 1 inch superior and 1 inch

1 inch lateral to the umbilicus bilaterally. Bonus high-yield item: the
